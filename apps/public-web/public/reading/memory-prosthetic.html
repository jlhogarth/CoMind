Memory as a prosthetic

What if a memory tool returned the context a person needs at the moment of choice, with consent and room for correction?

Concept essay · CoMind controlled disclosure brief, version 0.2, 30 September 2026 · No clinical or product efficacy claim.

The human problem

Imagine someone wants to write a thoughtful note to an old colleague after years apart. They remember the relationship but cannot recall the most recent conversation or why a particular date matters. A memory aid might surface a source-backed reminder before they respond. It should show where the information came from and let the person decide what to say. This fictional example describes a design goal, not a CoMind capability proven in clinical use.

The phrase “memory as a prosthetic” names an aspiration to support continuity and self-direction when recall is unreliable or attention is overloaded. It does not imply a medical device, a cure, or a substitute for professional care.

Why the idea has a research foundation

Philosophers Clark and Chalmers argued that external objects can participate in thinking when a person relies on them as part of an ongoing cognitive process. Distributed cognition research likewise studies how people, tools, and representations together carry work. These are conceptual foundations, not clinical tests of CoMind.

Clinical research on external memory aids has reported promising results for prospective memory, including remembering to carry out intended actions. A 2021 review found particularly positive outcomes for external aids, while noting that many studies were small. A 2023 review of electronic aids after traumatic brain injury found support for some retrieval and execution tasks and called for more controlled work and end-user involvement. Neither review establishes that a conversational AI is safe or effective for dementia or any other condition.

The design challenge

A reminder can be wrong, stale, or disclosed to the wrong person. A plausible account of a relationship can be more damaging than an admitted gap. A responsible memory aid needs consent, inspectable sources, correction and deletion controls, uncertainty, privacy boundaries, and the option to do nothing. Caregiver access should never silently displace the person's authority.

AI adds a separate research problem. Long-term conversation benchmarks show that models still struggle with temporal and causal details across many sessions. CoMind's question is whether a governed memory layer can improve those outcomes without making errors durable.

Questions worth testing

  • Does a source-backed reminder improve the person's own task outcome, or merely sound reassuring?
  • Can people detect and correct a mistaken or outdated memory?
  • How do consent and access rules work across family, care, and professional contexts?
  • Does benefit persist over time without creating dependence or loss of control?

See the controlled continuity protocol for an initial synthetic measurement design, and the broader agenda for human-centered questions.
