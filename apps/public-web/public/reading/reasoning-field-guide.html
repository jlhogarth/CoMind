CoMind / Field guide 01

From a claim to a justified next move

A practical way to reason when records disagree, a tool reports success, or a remembered fact no longer fits.

Original CoMind educational guide, November 2026. The worked scenario is fictional. This method is a design proposal and thinking practice, not evidence of improved human outcomes.

Begin with the action, not the argument

Suppose a team plans to announce a new public guide. The deployment job is green. A colleague says the page is missing. Another person remembers seeing it yesterday. The tempting question is “Who is right?” A better first question is “What action are we deciding?” Announcing the page, repairing a route, and rolling back a deployment have different evidence needs and different owners.

Write the action as a verb and an object: “Announce the new guide to readers.” Then ask whether it can be undone and what a mistake would cost. A draft announcement can be held. A public claim about an unavailable resource can waste attention and erode trust. This frames how much verification is proportional.

Layer one: observations with provenance

“The job is green” is an observation about a job. “The public page works” is a different claim. “I saw it yesterday” concerns a different time. Provenance means the source, time, path, and context needed to tell those statements apart. Record the exact URL and the response at the time of the check. A 404 at one path does not establish that the whole deployment failed; a successful upload does not establish that the intended path resolves.

In this example, collect the deployment identifier, the requested URL, its HTTP response, and the public page as a visitor sees it. Keep the contradictory reports. Erasing the 404 because it is inconvenient removes the very signal that can locate the defect. This is why reliable autonomy requires readback rather than a success flag alone.

Layer two: competing explanations

From those observations, several stories are possible: the route is wrong, the page was uploaded under another name, a cache or access rule differs, or the colleague checked an earlier address. These are hypotheses, not observations. Their value comes from producing different checks.

  • Wrong route: request the intended path and inspect the deployed asset list.
  • Different version: compare the deployment identifier and content hash with the commit intended for release.
  • Access or cache difference: repeat the request from a clean, unauthenticated browser and inspect response headers.
  • Stale report: ask for the timestamp and exact address of the failed request.

Do not add more possibilities merely to sound thorough. Choose a small set that changes what you would do next.

Layer three: a check that could change your mind

A real test has a possible result that would weaken your preferred explanation. If you believe the release is available, a fresh request to the exact public URL returning 404 should lower your confidence. If you believe it failed everywhere, a successful anonymous readback of the correct page should narrow the claim. “Look at the dashboard again” may only repeat the original observation. “Open the exact public URL in a new session and verify its content and styles” tests the user-facing outcome.

The check should be matched to the decision. Announcing a page needs evidence that readers can reach and understand it. A security claim needs tests of the actual access boundary. For consequential or difficult-to-reverse actions, a qualified reviewer or responsible owner must evaluate the evidence. The aid can organize a question; it cannot grant authority.

Layer four: update the belief, preserve the history

Imagine the readback confirms 404. The earlier “deployment succeeded” observation stays true in its narrow sense; the broader “the page is available” claim becomes unsupported. Correct the status, repair the route, and test again. Later, if the page returns 200 with the expected content, record the new observation and its time. The earlier failure remains useful history for diagnosis.

This is the difference between remembering and being governed by stale memory. A record can preserve what was believed without treating it as current truth. The public belief-update specification describes the behavior CoMind proposes when new evidence conflicts with prior state. The continuity protocol describes how such behavior might be compared in a controlled test. Neither page proves a human benefit already occurred.

Layer five: authority and a bounded move

The release owner decides whether to announce, fix, or roll back. A tester can report what they observed and repeat a check. An assistant can assemble the evidence and suggest the next verification. Those roles should remain distinct. The smallest defensible move here is to hold the announcement, ask the owner to confirm the intended URL and version, repair if necessary, and read the public page back before publishing the claim.

For your own decision, use the same pattern: name the action; record observations and their time; state an inference and one serious alternative; choose a check that might disprove your inference; identify the person who may decide; then take a reversible next step when possible. Try the Decision Clinic workbench to turn those layers into a memo on your own device.

Where the method can fail

An apparently precise source may still be wrong. A test may check the wrong URL or only one environment. A person may omit a contrary observation because it is uncomfortable. Confidence can remain high after the evidence changes. A checklist cannot detect those failures by itself. Invite correction, preserve source context, and revisit the decision when new evidence arrives. High-stakes legal, medical, or financial choices require qualified people and primary sources; this guide cannot replace them.
