The Decision Clinic

A small practice for the moment when your memory, a confident answer, and the latest evidence disagree.

Original CoMind educational exercise, November 2026. All cases are fictional. This is a reasoning aid, not a diagnostic tool or a claim that CoMind improves decisions.

Before you choose

For each case, pause at the question before opening the response. Try four sentences: What did I observe? What am I inferring? What would change my mind? Who can decide?

You can finish all three in a few minutes. The point is not to guess the “right” answer. It is to find the next check that makes action safer and clearer.

Case 01 / Continuity

The meeting that moved

Last week, a colleague said your review would be at 10:00. You remember it clearly. This morning, a calendar invitation from the organizer says 11:30. A note in your task list still says 10:00.

What would you do before telling the team the meeting time?

Open the reasoning path

Observation: the current invitation says 11:30; your note records an earlier time. Inference: the meeting may have moved, but an invitation can also be mistaken. Check: inspect the invitation’s update history or ask the organizer if the change matters. Authority: the organizer confirms the schedule; you can communicate the verified time.

A useful memory retains the old time as history without promoting it to the current fact. Explore cognitive continuity.

Case 02 / Evidence

“Deployment successful”

A deployment tool shows a green success message. A teammate opens the public page and gets a 404. The team needs to know whether the release is available.

Is the release complete? What evidence is missing?

Open the reasoning path

Observation: the deployment step completed, while the requested page failed for a visitor. Inference: the artifact may be uploaded but its route, asset, or publication state may be wrong. Check: request the exact public URL, inspect the response, then compare the deployed version and route with the intended release. Authority: the release owner decides whether to announce, repair, or roll back.

A green job is evidence of that job, not proof of the visitor’s experience. Explore reliable autonomy.

Case 03 / Human agency

The preference that changed

Months ago, a person asked for detailed daily updates. Today they say, “Please send one short weekly summary instead.” An assistant still has the earlier preference in memory.

Which instruction should shape the next update?

Open the reasoning path

Observation: the person has made a newer, direct request. Inference: the older preference is likely superseded for this kind of update. Check: clarify only if “weekly” or the scope is consequentially ambiguous. Authority: the person controls their preference. Record its time and scope so the old request remains history, not current instruction.

Memory should help people revise their choices. Read the public belief-update specification.

A decision card to keep

Use this when a decision feels obvious but the record may have shifted. Write a sentence for each line, on paper or in your own notes. Nothing on this page collects or sends your answers.

01Decision: What action is actually at stake?

02Observed: What can I point to, and when was it true?

03Inferred: What story am I adding to those observations?

04Check: What single observation would change my mind?

05Authority: Who has the right to decide or correct this?

If the cost of a wrong call is high, slow down and verify with the responsible person or source. For a reversible low-stakes choice, the card may only take a minute.

What did you notice?

Try the card on something real today: a deadline, a remembered preference, or a status marked “done.” Then revisit what changed. The interesting question is whether your next action follows the freshest justified evidence.
